# Main Study 1 materials

The following description is transcribed from the working manuscript. Complete original survey exports were not available. This is a manuscript description, not an executable survey or original survey export.

We used a between-participants design to compare the effect of agency and compliance on the dependent variables: retaliatory service sabotage behaviors. Participants were first told:

“We are interested in your opinions about a company called SerVest. Like many companies, SerVest’s mission is to make sure customers are treated in a professional, friendly, and patient manner. Currently, the company is working to improve operations.”

Next, participants were asked to take the role of an employee of SerVest and were told:

“SerVest has a Customer Service Policy for its employees. Here is an excerpt. ‘At SerVest, we acknowledge that personal values vary.’

Participants were then randomized into one of two conditions.

(1) In the Agency condition, the text continued:

‘SerVest trusts you to make company decisions that reflect your values.’

(2) In the Compliance condition, the text continued instead:

‘SerVest trusts you to not let your personal values direct how you make company decisions.’

Participants then read and responded to three vignettes involving poor treatment from customers (adapted from Wang et al., 2011), each beginning: “In customer service at SerVest today,”

(vignette 1) “a customer yelled at you, refused to listen to you, cut you off mid-sentence, used condescending language to you, and argued with you the whole time throughout the call.”

(vignette 2) “a customer thought they were more important than you, demanded special treatment, asked you to do things they could do by themselves, and was impatient.”

(vignette 3) “a customer vented their bad mood out on you, complained without reason, doubted your ability, and spoke aggressively to you.”

Measures

Following each vignette, participants were presented with the dependent variables, a list of five service sabotage behaviors (adapted from Wang et al., 2011):

(1) “hang up on the customer,”

(2) “intentionally put the customer on hold for a long period of time,”

(3) “purposefully transfer the customer to the wrong department,”

(4) “purposefully disconnect the call,” and

(5) “tell the customer that you fixed the issue, even though you didn’t fix it.”

Participants indicated with checkboxes what behaviors they thought the customer deserved and then, with separate checkboxes, which behaviors they would enact against the customer (i.e., “affirmed” behaviors). Variables for deserved and affirmed sabotage behavior were created for each vignette by summing the number of behaviors selected.

Finally, participants filled out a demographic survey and received monetary compensation. There was one manipulation check in Study 1: after reading SerVest’s policy, participants completed an open-ended item asking them to restate the policy they had just read; two independent coders made exclusions. A final decision was made by the authors, based on whether participants’ responses to this item indicated an understanding of the correct condition of SerVest’s policy. There was one attention check at the conclusion of the study: participants indicated whether they paid attention or were distracted; distracted participants were excluded.
